# Visual Directions

Use one dominant visual direction per asset. For a full video, combine at most two directions: one main style for the series and one accent style for emphasis or transitions.

## 1. Editorial finance restraint

Best for finance explainers, market concepts, macro topics, data-driven segments, and serious commentary.

Visual traits:

- magazine-like composition
- restrained color palette
- off-white, charcoal, muted ink, warm gray, graphite, soft gold, muted red accents
- clean symbolic illustrations
- subtle paper grain or print texture
- large negative space
- calm authority rather than excitement

Use for:

- title frames
- concept cards
- data chapter openers
- market mechanism explainers
- poster-like video covers

Avoid:

- glowing charts everywhere
- generic fintech dashboard look
- blue-purple neon gradients
- fake futuristic city backgrounds

## 2. Japanese white-space editorial

Best for restrained, high-end, reflective, or concept-driven visuals.

Visual traits:

- spacious composition
- small but precise focal object
- low saturation
- deliberate asymmetry
- quiet texture
- clear information hierarchy

Use for:

- clean backgrounds
- section title cards
- quote cards
- symbolic finance concepts
- documentary chapter separators

Avoid:

- random Japanese symbols
- meaningless red circles
- decorative brush strokes without topic relevance

## 3. Handmade explainer collage

Best for making finance concepts feel human, understandable, and less corporate.

Visual traits:

- paper cutouts
- pencil marks
- annotation arrows
- torn paper edges
- hand-drawn charts
- low-tech tactile elements
- slightly imperfect but controlled composition

Use for:

- transparent PNG cutouts
- explainers about debt, bubbles, risk, incentives, inflation, tradeoffs
- video overlays
- chapter transitions

Avoid:

- messy scrapbook overload
- childish doodles unless the video tone needs it
- uncontrolled clutter

## 4. Documentary archive mood

Best for documentary segments, history, institutions, biographies, crises, policy, and real-world context.

Visual traits:

- archival paper texture
- muted sepia, faded black, newspaper gray, deep green, aged ivory
- cropped documents, maps, stamps, silhouettes, timeline fragments
- restrained grain and imperfect edges
- serious but not gloomy

Use for:

- background plates
- historical explainer visuals
- documentary section cards
- map or timeline overlays

Avoid:

- fabricating specific historical evidence
- fake newspaper headlines that imply real sources
- excessive grunge or conspiracy-board aesthetics

## 5. Minimal data clarity

Best for charts, data graphics, numeric comparisons, and visual explanations.

Visual traits:

- clean grid
- few colors
- strong labels
- high contrast between data and background
- generous margins
- chart elements large enough for video
- optional paper or print texture for warmth

Use for:

- bar comparisons
- timeline cards
- factor breakdowns
- simple diagrams
- accurately drawn data elements, with optional generated non-data textures or symbols

Avoid:

- decorative chart junk
- tiny axis labels
- dense dashboards
- fabricated numbers or inaccurate geometry; real charts use explicit data, while non-quantitative mechanism illustrations are marked as schematic

## 6. High-contrast symbolic poster

Best for thumbnails, posters, chapter covers, or dramatic turning points.

Visual traits:

- one strong symbol
- bold scale contrast
- limited palette
- strong silhouette
- cinematic crop without photographic dependency
- restrained but memorable composition

Use for:

- video covers
- opening title cards
- crisis / conflict / turning point segments
- key concept posters

Avoid:

- overdramatic fire, lightning, neon, or action-movie clichés
- making finance topics look like generic sci-fi

## 7. Soft illustrated systems

Best for explaining abstract systems without becoming cold or corporate.

Visual traits:

- flat or semi-flat illustration
- soft geometry
- muted palette
- clean icon-like metaphors
- friendly but not childish

Use for:

- supply chain
- credit flow
- capital allocation
- policy transmission
- network diagrams

Avoid:

- stock-vector corporate people
- overly cheerful SaaS illustration clichés

## 8. Low-saturation newspaper halftone collage

Chinese alias: 低饱和报纸网点拼贴风.

Best for business biographies, finance documentaries, company investment maps, platform ecosystem explainers, consumer industry timelines, and local services / food delivery / hospitality / entertainment market visuals.

Visual traits:

- warm aged-paper background with generous negative space
- documentary portrait treatment in warm grayscale, sepia, or faded black
- newspaper halftone dots, scan grain, print wear, and slight misregistration
- paper cutout objects that feel clipped from finance magazines, old newspapers, receipts, maps, tickets, or business archives
- hand-drawn annotation lines, arrows, location pins, and chart traces
- mostly gray, ivory, charcoal, sepia, and light brown, with one theme-derived accent color
- optional secondary risk or warning color only when the topic calls for it; do not default to a fixed red or yellow

Use for:

- business founder or executive portrait backgrounds
- company history and investment landscape frames
- platform ecosystem maps
- documentary chapter visuals
- background plates with room for titles, subtitles, charts, and motion overlays
- transparent PNG cutouts such as paper scraps, delivery boxes, receipts, food items, tickets, buildings, maps, chart fragments, arrows, and pins

Avoid:

- glossy studio portraits, vivid skin tones, hard-edged HD cutouts, and beauty retouching
- ecommerce poster styling, saturated product photography, and catalog-like object layouts
- 3D render gloss, neon fintech dashboards, blue-purple gradients, glowing chart lines, and cyberpunk effects
- overcrowded scrapbook clutter that destroys the calm text zone
